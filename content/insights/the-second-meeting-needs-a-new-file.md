The day after APEC Shenzhen is when the first meetings turn into follow-ups. Leaders week packed calendars; Wang Yi’s Shenzhen briefing closed the public script; private rooms now ask who actually sent what they promised. At the same time, markets and policy kept moving — Anthropic’s public debut, US Commerce approvals for large AI-chip sales to Chinese firms, congressional pressure for tighter controls, and US actions against Chinese telecom names hours after the Trump–Xi meeting. Headlines refreshed overnight. Your second meeting cannot open on the same PDF.

A second meeting needs a new file because the first meeting created a ledger. Someone wrote down a question about the cap table, a China-operating sentence, a customer reference, a counsel note on export exposure, or a use-of-proceeds line in weeks. Coming back with unchanged slides tells the room the ledger was theatre. Serious processes notice. They do not always say so. They simply slow the clock, add a diligence partner, or stop answering Friday messages.

Send the promised artefact within the week even if it is smaller than you hoped. A corrected ownership page beats a perfect redesign that never arrives. A one-paragraph counsel clarification beats a fifty-page memo still in draft. A customer sentence on letterhead beats another logo slide. The second meeting should open on that progress: “Here is what we said we would send; here is what changed; here is what is still open and by which date.” That agenda is more persuasive than another walkthrough of vision.

Summit weeks produce a special failure mode. Founders leave the first coffee high on atmosphere — someone important nodded, someone mentioned a fund name, someone said “send me the deck.” They treat the follow-up as a chance to polish the same narrative with fresher geopolitics language. That is the wrong rewrite. The partner already has the narrative. What they need is evidence that your company updates when the world updates. If chip-sale approvals and telecom enforcement landed in the same forty-eight hours as your first meeting, your second packet should at least refresh the China-exposure and supply-chain pages those events will revive. Not a political essay. A factual map with dates.

If you need more time, say the date. Silence plus the same slides is how processes die politely. Write the email even when the artefact is late: name the open item, name the blocker, name the day it will land, and offer a shorter call if the full pack is not ready. Adults prefer an honest calendar to a mysterious delay. Mystery reads as avoidance. Avoidance reads as risk.

Build the new file as a delta, not as a reboot. Keep the one-pager that still frames the company. Replace the pages that answered questions badly. Add a cover note of eight to twelve lines that a partner can forward internally without joining the call. That cover note is often more important than slide seven. Internal forwards decide whether you get a third meeting. Make the forward easy.

Biotech and deep-tech teams should treat second-meeting files as scientific and operational updates, not as branding. New batch data, assay-transfer status, CRO timeline slips, or a changed manufacturing site belong on page one of the delta. Capital teams should lead with process facts: data-room index updates, related-party schedule cleanups, FPI analysis if relevant, and any change to the cash runway expressed in weeks. The point is the same across sectors. The room must see motion.

What not to bring is the unchanged “full story” deck with a new title slide dated today. Date stamps do not equal progress. Neither does a longer appendix. If nothing material changed, say that in one sentence and explain why the original answers still hold — then propose the next proof you will produce. Honesty about a quiet week is better than fake motion. Fake motion is discovered in diligence and remembered in partner meetings you are not invited to.

Use the post-summit window deliberately. Attention is still elevated, but the cameras have moved. That is when careful follow-ups land: short, factual, tied to the first meeting’s open questions, and light enough to read on a phone between two other calls. The founders who win second meetings after leaders week are not the ones with the most topical APEC adjectives. They are the ones who returned with a new file that proves the company keeps a ledger.

深圳亞太經合組織（APEC）剛結束的隔天，行程表上的「第一場見面」開始變成跟進。領袖週把日曆塞滿；王毅在深圳通報會議成果，公開劇本告一段落；私人房間接着問的是：誰真的寄出了答應過的東西。同一時段，市場與政策並未停——Anthropic公開亮相、美國商務部放行大額對華人工智能芯片、國會續推更緊管制，以及特朗普與習近平會面後數小時美方對中國電信企業採取行動。頭條隔夜就換。你的第二次會議，不該再用同一份PDF開場。

第二次見面需要新檔案，因為第一次見面已經留下一本賬：股權表問題、中國營運表述、客戶推薦、出口暴露的律師說明，或是以「週」計算的資金用途。若你帶着一成不變的投影片回來，等於告訴房間那本賬只是演技。認真的流程會察覺，卻未必當面說破——只是把時鐘放慢、加一名盡調合夥人，或不再回星期五的訊息。

答應過的材料，即使比預期小，也應在一週內寄出。更正過的股權頁，勝過永遠不到的完美重設計；一段律師澄清，勝過仍在草稿的五十頁備忘；客戶抬頭紙上的一句話，勝過又一頁標誌牆。第二次會議應從進度打開：「這是我們說會寄的；這是已變更的；這是仍未完成、以及截止日期。」這種議程，往往比再講一遍願景更有說服力。

峰會週還有一種特別的失敗模式。創辦人離開第一場咖啡時氣氛很好——重要的人點了頭、有人提了基金名字、有人說「把簡報寄我」。跟進於是變成用更新鮮的地緣政治措辭打磨同一套敘事。改錯地方了。對方已有敘事；他們要的是：世界在更新時，公司也會更新。若芯片放行與電信執法落在你首會後的同一四十八小時，第二包材料至少應刷新那些事件會重新喚醒的中國暴露與供應鏈頁。不要寫政治論文，要寫帶日期的事實圖。

若需要更多時間，就說出日期。沉默加上同一份投影片，是流程客氣地死去的方式。材料遲到也要寫信：點名未完成項目、卡點、預計落地日，並在全套未備齊時提議更短的電話。成年人寧可要老實日曆，也不要神秘拖延；神秘讀起來像迴避，迴避讀起來像風險。

新檔案宜做成「差額」，而不是整份重啟。仍能框住公司的一頁說明可以留；答得差的頁要換；再加八到十二行封面說明，讓合夥人不用進會也能對內轉發。這段封面說明常常比第七頁投影片更重要——內部轉發決定你有沒有第三次見面，轉發必須省力。

生技與深科技團隊，應把第二次檔案當成科學與營運更新，而不是品牌刷新：新批次數據、方法轉移進度、委託研究機構時程延誤、或生產基地變更，都該放在差額的第一頁。融資團隊則領先流程事實：資料室索引更新、關聯交易表清理、如適用的外國私人發行人分析，以及以週表達的現金跑道變化。跨行業的重點相同：房間必須看見你有進度。

最不該帶的，是只換了今日日期的「完整故事」舊簡報。日期戳不等於進度，更厚的附錄也不等於進度。若實質上沒有變化，就用一句話說清楚、解釋為何原答案仍成立，並提出下一步將交出的證明。安靜一週的老實話，好過假動作；假動作會在盡調被拆穿，並留在你未被邀請的合夥人會上被記住。

峰會後的窗口要刻意用。注意力仍高，鏡頭卻已移開——正是仔細跟進該落地的時候：短、具體、扣住首會未決問題，輕到能在兩通電話之間用手機讀完。領袖週之後贏得第二次見面的，不是APEC形容詞最多的人，而是帶着新檔案回來、證明公司真的有記賬的人。
